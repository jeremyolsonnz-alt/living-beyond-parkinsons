For family & friends

If someone you care about has Parkinson's, your steady presence matters more than anything on this site. Here's how these practices fit in, and how to help.

What they're using

What the practices actually are

The practices here are calm, guided imagery exercises. The person rests and gently pictures — or simply senses — easy movement, or a settled, calm feeling. Each one takes about fifteen minutes and is done sitting or lying down.

They're a gentle companion to medical care, not a treatment, and we're careful not to overstate them. Understanding that helps you support without expecting too much: the aim is ease and quality of life, not a cure or a fix.

How to help

Small things that genuinely help

  • Make space, don't push. Offer a quiet fifteen minutes and a calm room. Let them choose whether and when to practise. Pressure is the enemy of relaxation.
  • Let them lead. These practices are theirs. You don't need to join in or supervise unless they ask you to.
  • Help with the practical, not the personal. Reading a script aloud if their eyes tire, setting up the audio, or just handling interruptions can be a real kindness.
  • Watch your language. "Are you feeling better yet?" can quietly turn a gentle practice into a test they can fail. "Did you enjoy that?" is kinder.
  • Take the medical lead from professionals. Medication timing, physiotherapy, and speech therapy do the heavy lifting. Encourage those appointments; this site never replaces them.
Gentle cautions

A few things to hold lightly

Balance and walking need real-world care. Some practices rehearse movement in the mind only. They are never a reason to try walking or balancing without the usual support. If falls are a worry, a physiotherapist and a falls assessment are what matter — not imagery.

Imagery doesn't work the same for everyone, and that's fine. Some people picture things vividly; some feel movement without any picture; some experience neither. None of that is failure. If they say "nothing happened," the "when nothing happens" page may reassure you both.

Look after yourself

Supporting someone is tiring, too

Caring for someone with a long-term condition takes a quiet toll. Your wellbeing matters — for your own sake, and because you can only support well when you're supported too. Carer support groups and your GP can help. The calm practices on this site are for you as well, if you'd like them.
