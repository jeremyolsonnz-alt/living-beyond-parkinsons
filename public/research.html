The research, honestly

Why imagining movement might help with Parkinson's — how this site thinks about it, the brain science behind it, and an honest account of what's solid, what's shaky, and what we simply don't know yet.

The short version, in plain words

If you read nothing else

  • What this is: imagining a movement in your mind gently switches on some of the same brain areas as really moving. That's the idea behind these practices.
  • The honest bottom line: the most recent large study — one that pooled many trials together — found that imagery did not clearly improve people's overall movement scores. So we don't claim it improves your Parkinson's movements.
  • Where there's a little hope: for walking and "freezing," some smaller studies still point in a good direction — weakly, and mostly for watching movement rather than imagining it. That's the one practice we rate a step higher.
  • An honest complication: Parkinson's slows down imagined movement too, not just real movement. That's exactly why these practices are led by a voice instead of left to you.
  • What it's really for: feeling calmer and a bit more at ease, as a gentle add-on to your real care — medication, physiotherapy, speech therapy. Never instead of them.
  • Speech: for a soft voice there's a proper treatment (called LSVT LOUD). Imagery is not a stand-in, so we don't offer one.

The rest of this page explains all of that more fully, with the studies behind each point. Read as much or as little as you like.

The framing

Not recovery. Not only acceptance.

Here's an important difference this site starts from. Parkinson's gets worse slowly over time — it isn't a one-off injury you can simply relearn your way back from. So the word "recovery" — getting back to how things were — doesn't belong here. Nothing on this site will promise that.

But there's an opposite mistake too. Because Parkinson's gets worse over time, it's easy to think the only honest thing to offer is comfort and acceptance — with no hope that movement itself can improve at all. That would be too cautious. For some Parkinson's symptoms, there's real, published research showing that mental practice can genuinely help — not a lot, but really.

Where this site stands

Symptom management and quality of life, within a progressive condition

Not getting better in the sense of going backwards — but living better, and sometimes moving a little more easily, even while the condition itself keeps moving forward.

That's the whole claim, and nothing more. Every practice, every script, and every evidence rating on this site sticks to it — hopeful where the research allows it, and quiet where it doesn't.

The neuroscience

Why imagining a movement is not nothing

When you clearly imagine doing a movement, your brain doesn't just sit there. Picturing an action in detail switches on many of the same brain areas that switch on when you actually do it. Imagining a movement and actually making it are, to your brain, close cousins.

Researchers have used this to study Parkinson's itself. Brain scans of people imagining walking show unusual activity in the very same walking-control areas that are linked to freezing (Snijders et al., 2011). Imagining and moving really are close relatives in the brain.

The complication, said plainly

There's a catch here that doesn't come up with other conditions, and it would be dishonest to skip past it. Parkinson's slows down imagined movement too — not just real movement. When researchers ask people to imagine a task and time how long the imagined version takes, it comes out slowed in much the same way the real movement is (Heremans et al., 2012). How vivid someone's imagery is also varies from person to person, and appears to be connected to how severe their symptoms are (Readman et al., 2023).

Put simply: the very ability these practices rely on is itself affected by the condition they're meant to help with. That's an uncomfortable thing to admit on a site built around imagery. It's also true, so it belongs here.

The same research found something useful inside that problem. When people were given cues to guide the imagery, the slowing got smaller and the pictures got clearer (Heremans et al., 2012). That's the reason every practice on this site is led and paced by a voice, instead of leaving you to imagine on your own. With Parkinson's, guided imagery and unguided imagery are not the same thing — and the research suggests the guiding is the part that matters.

So the basic idea holds together: if imagining a movement uses real movement circuitry, and if the imagery is cued and guided so it stays clear, then practising an easy, comfortable movement in your mind might make that ease a little more available to your body. That's a reasonable idea — but an idea isn't proof, and the next section is where we get honest about what's actually been tested.

What the evidence shows

Solid in places, thin in others, absent in a few

Research into imagery for Parkinson's is still new, and it's patchy. Put simply: the basic brain-science case is fairly solid, a few small studies point in a hopeful direction but aren't very certain, at least one well-run study found no effect at all, and some symptoms have barely been studied. The main finding: no overall improvement in movement

The single most important thing to know is a disappointing one. A 2025 study pooled together many carefully done trials of motor imagery and watching movement, using the standard overall movement-symptom score doctors use for Parkinson's. It found no real improvement — whether people were on their medication or off it (Pettenuzzo et al., 2025). For overall movement symptoms, the most recent and most direct evidence says: no effect. Keep that in mind for everything below.

It isn't the only study to come up empty. An earlier trial run across several treatment centres — better designed than most in this field — compared mental practice against plain relaxation, and found mental practice was no better for people's mobility (Braun et al., 2011). That one is worth sitting with for a moment, because it can be read two ways: either mental practice didn't do much, or relaxation did just as much good. The study can't tell the difference, because nobody in it was given neither. Since this site offers both, the result is less damning than it first looks — but it's still a null result, and hiding it would defeat the point of this page.

Where there's still some hope: walking and freezing

Walking is the one area where something positive still shows up — but only when researchers measure walking itself, not overall movement scores. The brain-science reasoning is solid: imagining walking uses the same circuits involved in freezing. There's also a sensible argument that imagery could help someone rehearse a way through freezing triggers — like walking through a doorway — by practising where to place their attention or which cue to use (How, Wagner & Brach, 2022). Reviews focused on walking report small, uncertain improvements in walking speed and in freezing episodes afterward. Several studies have reported fewer freezing episodes after training built around watching movement, including one where brain scans changed alongside the walking (Pelosin et al., 2010; Agosta et al., 2017). But the one well-run trial that combined watching movement with imagining it found no effect on freezing or balance at all (Bezerra et al., 2022).

That difference deserves naming, because it doesn't flatter this site. The encouraging freezing results mostly come from watching movement on video. The practices here are about imagining movement. The two are related — the brain treats them as cousins — but they aren't the same thing, and evidence for one doesn't automatically carry across to the other. If freezing is your main worry, video-based observation training and cueing strategies from a physiotherapist have more behind them than anything on this site does, and that's where to start.

That fragile, carefully-hedged bit of hope is the entire reason Walking Freely is rated a step higher than the other four practices. It isn't a strong reason. It's simply the strongest one available.

Once promising, now downgraded: slowness of movement

Slowness of movement (bradykinesia) used to be the most hopeful area — trials found that adding motor imagery to physiotherapy improved slowness more than physiotherapy alone (Kashif et al., 2022). Two things have since weakened that finding. Those trials mixed imagery together with virtual reality, so no one could tell how much imagery alone was doing. And the big 2025 study above, which directly measured movement symptoms including slowness, found nothing. One cleaner study did speed up finger movement after a single session of watching movement, and the effect was still measurable two days later (Pelosin et al., 2013). It's worth knowing what that was measured against: not a blank screen, but a steady sound cue — an approach already known to help people with Parkinson's. That's a demanding comparison to come out ahead of, which makes the result more interesting than it first appears. It was still a small study measuring finger tapping in a lab, not anything in daily life. This is why Finding Flow's rating was lowered.

The honest gap: speech

This site doesn't offer a guided-imagery practice for speech, and that's on purpose. Soft, unclear speech in Parkinson's (called hypophonia) already has a proven, gold-standard treatment — LSVT LOUD — which works through intensive, effortful retraining of how loudly a person speaks. That's a completely different approach from mental rehearsal, and it gets results that last (Ramig et al., 2001; Fox et al., 2012). Offering a gentle imagery script alongside it would wrongly suggest the two are equally effective. If speech is a concern, the honest advice is to see a speech-language therapist — not to use a script from this site.

There is one related thing on offer here, and the difference is worth being precise about. A one-to-one conversation with Jeremy can make room for how a changing voice feels — going quiet in a group, not bothering to repeat yourself, letting someone else speak for you, the slow retreat from conversations you used to enjoy. That's the social and emotional side, and it isn't what LSVT LOUD sets out to treat. It also isn't a replacement for LSVT LOUD. No conversation will make your voice louder; only the voice therapy does that.

The non-negotiable

A complement, never a replacement

Whatever the research does or doesn't show, one rule sits above all of it. Guided imagery is offered here alongside the things that actually do the heavy lifting in Parkinson's care — medication and its careful timing, physiotherapy, cueing strategies, speech therapy, and the guidance of a Parkinson's team. It never replaces any of them, and every practice page says so in its own words.

If a practice ever starts to feel like a replacement for your medical care, that's a sign to step back, not to lean in further. This site is meant to sit quietly alongside good treatment — not compete with it.

One more honesty note

On the four-part structure

The separate ingredients used on this site — imagining movement, watching movement, relaxing, and breathing exercises — each have research behind them, listed in the bibliography. But the specific way they're combined into a four-part session — Part I (Breath and Light), Part II (The Ideal Being), Part III (The Anatomical Pathway), and Part IV (Closing) — is Jeremy's own creation, based on a therapy approach developed by Milton Erickson and his followers. That whole four-part combination has never been tested together in a clinical trial, and every practice page says so clearly. It's reasonable to trust the individual ingredients; it wouldn't be reasonable to assume the whole recipe has been proven.
