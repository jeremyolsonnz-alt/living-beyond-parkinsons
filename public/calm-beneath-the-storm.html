Calm Beneath the Storm

A practice for the anxious hours, the restless nights, and the distress that can come when a dose wears off — a way of meeting the hard moments differently, not a way of making them disappear.

Frequency  ·  Whenever you need it Evidence  ·  Emerging — indirect, borrowed from broader mindfulness research
What this practice helps with

Anxiety, "off"-period distress, and restless sleep

Parkinson's carries more than movement. Many people live with anxiety, with the low grey pull of a difficult day, with sleep that won't settle, and with the particular distress of an "off" period — those stretches when medication has worn thin and the body feels least like your own.

This practice does not try to fix any of that. It offers something quieter: a familiar, repeatable place to return to when the day is loud — a way of loosening the grip of a hard moment so that it can be lived through rather than fought.

An honest word about "off" periods. This practice will not shorten an off period or change your medication cycle — those follow their own pharmacological clock, and nothing here alters it. What a settling practice can offer is a gentler way to be inside those stretches: less alarm layered on top of the physical difficulty, and a steadier place to wait from. Your medication schedule, and any changes to it, belong with your neurologist and Parkinson's nurse.

Shape of the session

The four parts of this practice

For why this method works and how the four parts fit together, see The Four Parts on the Guided Imagery page.

Part I · Breath and Light
What it's for

For anxious or overwhelmed moments, this settling isn't a warm-up for what's coming — it's already most of the point.

How it works

A few unhurried minutes of noticing the breath as it already is, and letting a soft warmth or light rest wherever it's easy — no figure yet, nothing to become.

Part II · The Settled Place
What it's for

Calm isn't an action to rehearse, so unlike the other four practices, this one doesn't ask you to watch a figure and become a calmer version of yourself — that could carry the unwanted message that where you are now is wrong. Instead, it simply arrives at a settledness that's already yours.

How it works

A place that's already calm — real or imagined — is brought to mind, and you simply notice you're already there. If the storm is still going on, it's allowed to carry on at the edge of things, like weather outside a window, while you rest beneath it.

Part III · The Quieting Body
What it's for

Knowing that the body has its own built-in quieting system — one that responds to breath, not effort — makes this rehearsal make sense as something physiological, not just comforting words.

How it works

A plain-language look at how a slow, long out-breath invites the body's own quieting response — shown as something that spreads, rather than as a route traced through the body.

This is a conceptual diagram, not an anatomical illustration. Nothing in it traces a nerve or a route through the body, because that isn’t what is being shown. The rings stand for a response that spreads rather than travels, and the gauge is a figure of speech drawn out. None of it is under voluntary control: the practice makes room for the response, it doesn’t perform it.
Part IV · Closing
How it works

A slow return to the ordinary sounds of the room. Nothing to achieve and nothing to get back to — you met a hard moment and stayed with yourself through it, which is the whole of it.

The Script

Read this to yourself slowly — allow about 30–45 seconds per paragraph — or use it as the text for the audio narration above.

Read the Script

Part I — Breath and Light

And you're here now, reading or listening. You've already taken the time to be here, which is its own small kindness, and there's nothing more you need to do to have begun.

Perhaps you're sitting. Perhaps you're lying down. Perhaps your eyes are still open, and perhaps in a moment they'll feel like closing on their own. There's no hurry in any of it — you can take this at exactly the pace that suits you.

You're breathing. You've been breathing all along, without having to think about it — the body has been quietly doing that for you the whole time. And now, if you like, you might just notice it. Not changing anything. Only noticing the breath that's already there.

And you may find, as people often do, that simply letting the breath be noticed lets it lengthen a little on its own. The out-breath, especially — long, and unhurried, like something being set down.

And somewhere — in the room, behind your eyes, or in a memory of a warm afternoon — there's a soft light and warmth. You don't have to summon it sharply. Just let it rest somewhere it's easy to rest: the shoulders, perhaps, or the hands, or the slow rise and fall of the chest.

Part II — The Settled Place

Somewhere in you — or somewhere you can bring to mind — there's a place that is already calm. It doesn't need building. It's more like a room you already have, that's been quietly waiting, with the light already on.

It might be a real place you've been: a shoreline, a garden, a kitchen at a certain hour, a seat by a window. Or it might be nowhere in particular — just the feeling of a place where nothing is being asked of you.

And you don't have to travel to it, or earn your way in. You can simply notice that you're already there. The ground holding you. The air a comfortable temperature. The quiet not empty, but easy.

If the storm is still going on somewhere — the anxious thought, the tremor, the difficult hour — you can let it carry on at the edge of things, the way weather carries on outside a window. You don't have to make it stop to be here. You're beneath it now, in the settled place, and it can do what it does without needing your hand on it.

So you rest here for a while. Nothing to reach for. Nothing to fix. Just a person, in a calm place, breathing — which turns out to be enough.

Part III — The Quieting Body

The body has a quieting system of its own — one that doesn't need instructions, only a little space to work. As the breath slows, that system begins, gently, to answer.

And it seems to be the out-breath that matters most — not the drawing in, but the letting go. A long, unhurried out-breath is read by the body as a sign that there's no emergency here, and the body answers it in its own quiet way. The heart slows a touch. The muscles that were braced let go a fraction. The belly softens. The alarm turns itself down.

You don't have to make this happen. You couldn't force it if you tried — that's rather the point of it. All you're doing is giving it room: one slow breath, and then another, each long out-breath making a little more space, and the body, in its own time, beginning to answer back.

And whatever is difficult in the body today — the stiffness, the restlessness, the wearing-off — you're not fighting it here. You're just letting the quieting system do the one thing it knows how to do, alongside everything else that's going on.

Part IV — Closing

And when you're ready — in your own time, with no signal to wait for — you can let the ordinary sounds of the room come back. The surface beneath you. The light in the space you're actually in.

You don't have to carry the whole calm out with you. Just a little of it, however much came — a slightly longer breath, a slightly looser shoulder, a sense that the settled place is still there and can be returned to whenever the day gets loud again.

Your body moves through natural rhythms all day. About every 90 minutes, attention naturally softens — when you notice that drift, or whenever the day gets loud, you can return for a breath or two to the settled place. At night, as you drift toward sleep, this is often the most natural time to return to it — whatever you're holding in mind can carry on quietly working while you rest.

There was nothing to achieve here, and nothing to get back to. You didn't fail if the storm kept going, and you didn't have to fix anything to have done this well. You met a hard moment, and you stayed with yourself through it — which is the whole of it.

And whenever you need it, the settled place will be here. It doesn't run out.

When to Practise

This practice is different from the other four in one more way: there's no on-waking window suggested for it. Rest and downregulation aren't something to prescribe first thing, before the day's demands have even arrived — this is a practice for when you actually need settling, not a morning routine. Two windows tend to work especially well.

Throughout the Day
Every 90 minutes or so, attention naturally softens — you might notice it as a dip in focus or a pull to pause. When you notice that drift, or whenever the day gets loud, spend two or three breaths returning to the settled place. It takes less than a minute and it counts.
Before Sleep
The minutes before sleep are when the mind is most receptive, and this is often the most natural time for this particular practice. Bring the settled place to those minutes — whatever you're holding in mind can carry on quietly working while you rest.

An honest note. Two strands of research sit behind this practice, and they're not equally strong. The clearer one is for mindfulness and meditation broadly: several randomised trials in Parkinson's have found reductions in anxiety and depressive symptoms. That evidence is for mindfulness programmes as a category, not for this specific guided-imagery script, so it supports the idea of a settling practice more than it validates these exact words. The second strand is thinner: a small home-based study found relaxation guided imagery (but not relaxing music) was associated with more daily "on" time — an intriguing but small and preliminary signal, not proof that imagery increases on-time or replaces any part of medical management. Full references are listed on the Bibliography page.

The specific four-part structure used here — Part I (breath and light), Part II (the Settled Place), Part III (the Quieting Body), and Part IV (closing) — is Jeremy's own creation. Nobody has tested this exact combination in a clinical trial. Each piece on its own is backed by research; putting them together like this is a reasoned choice, not a proven method.

This is not a treatment. It's a free practice, meant to sit alongside — never replace — your medication, physiotherapy, speech therapy, and the guidance of your Parkinson's team.
