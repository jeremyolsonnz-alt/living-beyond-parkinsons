—  Practice 4  ·  The Lightest Evidence Of The Motor Practices

The Steady Hand

A mental rehearsal of the hand that stays full and fluid — the writing that keeps its size, the fingers that find the button — practised in imagination, as a companion to the occupational therapy that does the practical work.

Duration  ·  15 minutes Frequency  ·  Daily is a reasonable start Evidence  ·  Emerging — small, preliminary studies
What this practice helps with

Micrographia and fine motor difficulty

Parkinson's often reaches the hands in small, daily ways. Handwriting can start at a normal size and then tighten and shrink across a line or a page — a pattern called micrographia. Buttons, zips, cutlery, and coins can turn a quick task into a slow, effortful one. Of the motor practices, hand function has the least evidence behind it — the relevant studies are small and preliminary, from a single research group, and honestly badged as such.

This practice rehearses the opposite quality: a hand that moves fluidly, and writing that keeps its full size all the way along. These are the two things micrographia most reliably takes away, and the two things this practice rehearses — a hand that stays large and easy, not one that gradually tightens.

This is mental rehearsal — picture it, don't perform it. You'll be imagining the movements while your hands rest, not writing or manipulating objects during the practice. It's a rehearsal to do at rest, then carry into the real tasks separately.

Occupational therapy is the practical partner here. For hand function, handwriting, and daily tasks, an occupational therapist is the professional who can help most — with amplitude and handwriting strategies, adapted tools, and tailored exercises. Ask your GP or neurologist for a referral if fine-motor difficulties affect your day. This imagery practice sits alongside that work; it doesn't replace it, and it doesn't replace your medication, which often eases hand slowness as part of your dose cycle.

Shape of the session

The four parts of this practice

For why this method works and how the four parts fit together, see The Four Parts on the Guided Imagery page.

Part I · Breath and Light
≈ 3 min
What it's for

Micrographia often means small, careful, tightened movements, so settling first — with the hands doing nothing at all — is the opposite of that guarded quality.

How it works

A few unhurried minutes of settled breathing and a soft warmth in the palms and fingers — no writing imagery yet, just the hands resting.

Part II · The Ideal Being
≈ 5 min
What it's for

The figure isn't a memory of your old handwriting — there's no former hand to get back to. It's rehearsed specifically at the size of the movement, since staying full and open is what micrographia takes away first.

How it works

A figure is watched writing and handling ordinary objects with full, fluid ease — the letters staying open, the fingers finding buttons and cups without fuss. Then the figure steps into you, so that fullness becomes yours from the inside.

Part III · The Working Hand
≈ 4 min
What it's for

Knowing that movement size responds to deliberate intention — not just medication — is why aiming for fullness in rehearsal is worth doing at all.

How it works

A plain-language look at how much of the brain is devoted to the hand, why movements tend to shrink in Parkinson's, and how deliberately rehearsing large, full movements can help push back against that shrinking.

The Full Script

Read the Full Script

Part I — Breath and Light

And you're here now, settled, your hands resting and asked to do nothing at all. Let them be heavy, or light, or somewhere in between — however they happen to be is exactly right.

There's a little time set aside, and nothing to accomplish in it. Just the rehearsing of ease, gently, while the hands themselves rest.

You're breathing, easily and on its own. And you might let your attention drift, in a moment, down the arms and into the hands — those remarkable, expressive things that do so much of our reaching out into the world.

A soft warmth, settling into the palms and the fingers. Not a task, not a stretch — just warmth, and ease, resting in the hands like sun on a windowsill.

Part II — The Ideal Being

A little way off, there's a figure — sitting at a table, writing. You may see them clearly, or simply sense them there. And notice the hand. It moves fluidly, easily, the pen gliding along without gripping or cramping. And notice, especially, the size of the writing: full and open, each letter as generous as the last, staying that way all the way along the line. Nothing shrinking, nothing tightening. The hand simply keeps its fullness, effortlessly.

And now the figure sets down the pen and does something ordinary — fastens a button, perhaps, or picks up a cup. Notice how the fingers find their way without fuss: smooth, unhurried, sure. Not fast, not forced. Just easy, full movements that do what they mean to.

This is the quality to stay with: not speed, and not precision under pressure, but fullness and flow — the hand moving at its natural, ample size, relaxed and continuous. Writing that stays large. Fingers that stay easy.

And now the figure turns, and comes closer, and steps into you — gently — so that being near it becomes doing, from the inside.

Take in your own hand moving that way. An imagined pen, gliding — the letters full and open, keeping their size across the line. An imagined button, an imagined cup — the fingers easy and sure. Not forcing anything. Just rehearsing a hand that stays full and fluid. However your hands are today, you've taken in the ease of them, and that's yours to keep.

Part III — The Working Hand

A great deal of the brain is given over to the hand — far more than its size would suggest. Finger movements are shaped by brain areas that plan and size up movement, drawing on the same deep systems that help keep any movement at its full size. In Parkinson's, when those systems run short of dopamine, movements tend to shrink — and the hand, which trades in small, precise movements, feels it keenly. That shrinking of the handwriting has a name — micrographia — but here it's simply the tightening grip we're gently working against.

Here's the useful part: movement size responds to intention and to attention. When you deliberately aim for large, full movements — in rehearsal, and then in practice with your therapist — you push back against the shrinking. Aiming big helps the movement land at a more normal size.

So bring the full, easy hand to mind once more — the writing keeping its size, the fingers moving amply and without cramp. Not straining. Just holding the intention of fullness, quietly, so the pattern of a generous movement stays warm and familiar, ready for the real page and the real button, and for the exercises your occupational therapist gives you.

Part IV — Closing

And when you're ready, in your own time, let the room return, your hands resting where they are. No need to test anything, and no rush to reach for a pen.

Carry a little of it with you, however much came — the sense of a hand that stays full and easy, the intention of writing that keeps its size. It's a pattern you've rehearsed, and it will be here to rehearse again.

There was nothing to get back to here, and nothing to prove. This practice sits gently beside the real work of the hands — your occupational therapy, your own daily practice, your medication. It's a rehearsal of ease, offered to your hands as they are today. And whenever you'd like it, the full, easy hand is here to bring to mind again.

When to Practise

Once a day is a reasonable, sustainable starting point — there's no strict dose established for this practice, so let it fit wherever it fits in your day. The formal session isn't the only practice time; three other windows each day are also available.

Throughout the Day
Every 90 minutes or so, attention naturally softens — you might notice it as a dip in focus or a pull to pause. When you notice that drift, spend two or three breaths returning to the full, open feeling of the hand at rest. It takes less than a minute and it counts.
Before Sleep
The minutes before sleep are when the mind is most receptive. Bring the feeling of a hand that stays full and fluid to those minutes — whatever you're holding in mind can carry on quietly working while you rest.
On Waking
Stiffness and slower movement are often strongest first thing, before your morning medication has settled in. A few unhurried minutes here, before you're up, is a gentle way to rehearse ease in the hands before the day begins.

An honest note. Of the motor practices, hand function has the least evidence behind it. A handful of studies from one research group have shown that combining action observation and motor imagery can influence the amplitude of hand movements in Parkinson's — a plausible route to the reduced-size problems behind micrographia — and have developed a home-based observation-and-imagery programme for functional hand movements, designed together with people who have Parkinson's. These are encouraging early steps: experimental and feasibility work, small samples, not yet definitive efficacy trials. There is no promise that this will improve handwriting or fine-motor tasks on its own — the occupational therapist remains the one who can actually help with those. Full references are listed on the Bibliography page.

The specific four-part structure used here — Part I (breath and light), Part II (the Ideal Being), Part III (the Working Hand), and Part IV (closing) — is Jeremy's own creation. Nobody has tested this exact combination in a clinical trial. Each piece on its own is backed by research; putting them together like this is a reasoned choice, not a proven method.

This is not a treatment. It's a free practice, meant to sit alongside — never replace — your occupational therapy, medication, and the guidance of your Parkinson's team.
