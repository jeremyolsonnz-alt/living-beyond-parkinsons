Plain-language overview

What this research is about

In Parkinson's, the cells that produce dopamine in a small region deep in the brain gradually die. Dopamine is what the brain's movement circuitry uses to start a movement and to set its size. Without enough of it, movements become slow, small and effortful, tremor can appear, and walking can suddenly stop altogether — the experience known as freezing.

The part of this that people notice first is often the strangest. Movements that used to happen by themselves — swinging an arm, taking a step, writing your signature — stop being automatic. They still work, but only if you consciously attend to them. This is why so many things that help in Parkinson's are essentially attention tricks: counting steps, marching to a beat, stepping over a line on the floor.

The research on this page asks whether attention itself can be trained. If watching a movement, imagining a movement, or deliberately calming the body can reach the motor system through a route that does not depend on the damaged dopamine circuit, then these might be useful additions to conventional treatment.

The idea behind it

Watching someone perform an action, and imagining performing it yourself, both activate motor areas of the brain without any movement taking place. The proposal in Parkinson's is specific: these routes run mainly through the outer layers of the brain — premotor and parietal cortex — which are relatively spared in Parkinson's, and so may partly bypass the damaged circuitry deeper down (Caligiore et al., 2017; Abbruzzese et al., 2015).

That is a hypothesis about mechanism, and it is worth saying so plainly. It explains why the idea is attractive; it does not by itself demonstrate that it works.

An unusual complication

There is a wrinkle here that does not arise in other conditions. Parkinson's appears to slow imagined movement as well as real movement. When people with Parkinson's are asked to imagine a task and the time is measured, the imagined movement is slowed in much the same way the physical one is (Heremans et al., 2012). The imagination is affected by the very thing it is being asked to treat.

The same study found something useful, though. When visual cues were provided during the imagery, the slowing reduced and the images became more vivid. Imagery ability also varies between individuals and appears to relate to symptom severity (Readman et al., 2023), and can be measured formally (Malouin et al., 2007). All of this suggests that guided imagery for Parkinson's should be cued and structured rather than left entirely to the person's own imagination.

What the studies found — movement

The first trial combined motor imagery with physical practice in a group setting: twelve people received both, eleven received physical practice alone, twice a week for twelve weeks. The combined group ended up faster at movement sequences and scored better on the standard Parkinson's rating scale. The authors noted the approach extends practice time at low cost and negligible risk (Tamir et al., 2007). It was a small study.

Watching movement has shown short-term effects. A single session of watching video of repetitive finger movements increased people's spontaneous movement speed, and the effect was still measurable two days later — while watching video of a static hand did nothing (Pelosin et al., 2013). Combining observation with imagery has been shown to influence the size of hand movements (Bek et al., 2019), and a home training programme built around this approach has been developed with patients themselves (Bek et al., 2021). Trials pairing motor imagery with virtual reality have reported gains in balance, motor function and daily activities (Kashif et al., 2022a, 2022b).

Against this sit two results that must be given equal weight. A randomised trial found that action observation and motor imagery had no effect on balance or freezing of gait (Bezerra et al., 2022). And the most recent meta-analysis pooled the trials that used the standard motor rating scale as their main outcome — six studies, 152 people in total — and found no difference between treatment and comparison groups, whether tested on or off medication (Pettenuzzo et al., 2025).

What the studies found — tremor, fluctuations and mood

The picture is different, and in some ways stronger, for the calming side of this work.

In twenty people with moderate to severe tremor, relaxation guided imagery reduced tremor dramatically when measured with an accelerometer, and abolished it entirely for between one and thirteen minutes in fifteen of them. Tremor remained below baseline half an hour later, and participants reported the benefit lasting several hours. Relaxing music helped less; attempting to relax without guidance did not help at all (Schlesinger et al., 2009). A later study from the same group reported reduced motor fluctuations (Schlesinger et al., 2014).

For mood, a randomised trial published in JAMA Neurology compared mindfulness yoga against stretching and resistance training and found greater improvement in anxiety and depression (Kwok et al., 2019). A mindfulness programme has been associated with structural changes visible on brain scans (Pickut et al., 2013), and a small uncontrolled study reported improvements in anxiety, depression, cognition and some motor measures (Dissanayaka et al., 2016).

Voice work sits alongside all of this as an established, separately evidenced treatment, with lasting benefits demonstrated at two years and measurable changes in brain activity (Ramig et al., 2001; Narayana et al., 2010; Fox et al., 2012).

What the research does not prove

The honest position has four parts.

First, on the standard measure of Parkinson's motor symptoms, the pooled evidence for imagery and observation is null (Pettenuzzo et al., 2025). Six trials and 152 people is a very small body of evidence, and the individual encouraging studies are mostly small and short.

Second, the freezing and balance results are actively negative, not merely uncertain (Bezerra et al., 2022). Freezing is among the most distressing symptoms, and it would be wrong to imply that this approach addresses it.

Third, the relaxation findings, striking as they are, come from small and unblinded studies of an outcome — tremor — that is known to respond to attention and stress. That does not make the effect unreal; anyone with Parkinson's knows what stress does to their tremor. It does mean the size of the effect should be treated cautiously.

Fourth, and most importantly, none of this is a substitute for exercise or for medication. The largest evidence base in Parkinson's rehabilitation covers physical exercise: 154 trials and nearly 7,800 people, showing benefit for motor symptoms and quality of life across almost every type of exercise studied, with little to distinguish one type from another (Ernst et al., 2024). The intervention with the strongest evidence in Parkinson's is moving, not imagining.

What it means

Two rather different claims are gathered on this site, and they deserve to be held to different standards.

Imagery and observation for movement are mechanistically plausible and supported by some encouraging small trials, but the pooled evidence on the standard motor scale does not yet show a benefit. It is reasonable to try; it would not be reasonable to promise.

Imagery and mindfulness for tremor, fluctuations, anxiety and mood rest on more consistent ground, including a randomised trial in a major neurology journal. Stress makes Parkinson's symptoms worse in a way people recognise immediately in their own bodies, and practices that reduce it are addressing something real.

What both share is a favourable balance of risk. No harm has been reported in any of these studies. They cost nothing, need no equipment, and can be practised on days when a walk is not possible. Used alongside exercise, medication and your clinical team — not in place of them — they are a reasonable thing to add.

This overview summarises published research and is not medical advice. Never change medication or treatment without speaking to your neurologist or Parkinson's nurse.
