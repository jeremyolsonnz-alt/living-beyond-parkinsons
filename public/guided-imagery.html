The Guided Imagery Programme

Five practices. One method. It's built on research showing that imagining a movement switches on many of the same brain networks as actually doing it — which may help make that movement a little more available, alongside your medication and physiotherapy.

Guided imagery is a set of mental practices you do alongside your usual Parkinson's care. In each one, you imagine a movement or state in detail — from the inside, with the feeling of it actually happening — and, in most practices, follow the pathway involved in the body. Here's why that may help: when you imagine a movement clearly, your brain switches on many of the same networks it uses to actually make that movement. Rehearsing this way, again and again, may help keep an easier, fuller version of that movement a little more available to your body — not by reversing Parkinson's, but by keeping a pattern of ease warm and familiar.

The practices are free. They are not a treatment, and they don't replace medication, physiotherapy, or the guidance of your Parkinson's team. They sit alongside those — something you can do on your own, in the gaps between appointments, or on the days when movement itself is harder.

How the method works

The Four Parts

Every practice follows the same four parts, in the same order. The order matters — each part sets up the one after it.

I
Part I · Breath and Light

Settling down

Every practice starts by settling you. You bring your attention to your body, slow your breathing, and let a soft warmth or light rest wherever it's easy. This calms the nervous system — worth doing deliberately in Parkinson's, where hurry and anxiety are common triggers for symptoms like freezing. Around three minutes.

II
Part II · The Ideal Being

Watch, then become

A figure appears — or you simply sense one — moving easily, doing the movement you're working on. First you watch them, and notice how smooth and easy it looks. Then the figure steps into you, and you become them. What you were watching, you now feel from the inside. Watching a movement wakes up many of the same brain areas as doing it; stepping in lets you rehearse the feeling of it yourself. Around four to five minutes.

III
Part III · The Pathway

Understand the pathway

Now the practice moves inward, to a plain-language picture of what's happening in the body — different for each practice, since Parkinson's affects several different systems. It's not a diagram to memorise, just enough to make the rehearsal make sense. Around four minutes.

IV
Part IV · Closing

Carry it back

You come back to your breath and let the images settle, carrying a little of the practised ease with you. Nothing to prove and no test passed or failed — then you bring yourself gently back to the room. Around two to three minutes.

Why this order?

Each part sets up the next. Part I settles the nervous system and gets you ready to focus. Part II wakes the movement system by watching, before you step in and feel it from the inside. Part III aims that rehearsal at the specific pathway this practice is working with. Part IV lets it settle before you return to the room. The parts are designed to build on one another, so doing them in order is how the practice is intended to work — though, like the four-part structure itself, that sequencing hasn't been tested on its own in any trial.

The figure, and one deliberate exception

The heart of each motor practice is Part II: a figure appears at a distance, is watched moving with ease, and then "steps into you," so that the feeling of easeful movement is borrowed from the inside. The figure is never a pre-Parkinson's self to chase back — there's no going back, and the practices never imply it. It's simply an embodiment of ease, offered to the body as it is now.

The one exception is Calm Beneath the Storm, the non-motor practice. Calm isn't an action to rehearse, and asking you to "become" a serene figure could carry an unwanted message that where you are now is wrong. So that practice sets the figure aside and simply arrives at a settled place directly — a restorative exception, made on purpose.

The practices

Five Areas, Five Practices

Pick the practice that fits where you are right now. You can do more than one, but it's usually best to get comfortable with a single practice for a week or two before adding another. They're separate — you don't have to do them in order.

Practice 1

Walking Freely

Gait & freezing of gait

Rehearsing the smooth approach and the way through a doorway, for the step that catches.
Practice 2

Finding Flow

Movement initiation & bradykinesia

For the pause before movement and the shrinking of it — rehearsing an easy, full-sized start.
Practice 3

Steady Ground

Balance & postural stability

A gentle, seated rehearsal of calm, unhurried steadiness — a quiet companion to physiotherapy.
Practice 4

The Steady Hand

Fine motor & handwriting

For writing that shrinks and fingers that fumble — rehearsing a hand that stays full and fluid.
Practice 5

Calm Beneath the Storm

Non-motor wellbeing — anxiety, "off"-period distress, sleep

A settling practice for the hard hours — meeting the storm differently, not making it disappear.
When to practise

How Often — and the Best Times

There's no required schedule. Once a day is a reasonable place to start, fitting the practice wherever it fits in your day. But the full fifteen-minute session isn't the only time it can help — three moments in the day tend to work especially well for rehearsing ease, and even a few slow breaths returning to a practice's main image, without doing the whole session, keeps the pattern close at hand.

On Waking
Stiffness and slower movement are often strongest first thing, before your morning medication has settled in. A few unhurried minutes here, before you're up, is a gentle way to rehearse ease before the day begins.
Throughout the Day
Any pause before you know you'll be doing the thing a practice rehearses — moving through a doorway, starting to write, standing up. A few slow breaths back to that image keeps it close at hand.
Before Sleep
A calm way to close the day. Not about tomorrow's movements — just a settling practice, and a natural place for the mind to rest on something easy.

If you've been practising Walking Freely, come back to the line on the floor and the step over it, for two or three breaths. If you've been practising Finding Flow, come back to the feeling of a movement starting the moment you intend it. If you've been practising Steady Ground, come back to the easy sway and settle of standing. If you've been practising The Steady Hand, come back to the full, open feeling of the hand at rest. If you've been practising Calm Beneath the Storm, come back to the settled place you found there. These small moments add up.

Getting started

How to Begin

Pick one practice. Play the audio if that helps — or read the script to yourself, or have someone read it to you. Get into a comfortable, supported position — seated or lying down both work well; this is mental rehearsal, not movement, so there's no wrong position to start from. Most people close their eyes; softly gazing at a point in the distance also works.

The first session may feel unfamiliar, and that's completely normal — it often takes a few tries before guided imagery starts to feel more natural. If the imagery feels faint, effortful, or simply doesn't arrive at first, that's a known feature of the territory, not a failure on your part — most studies of motor imagery only recruit people who can already form vivid mental pictures, and you haven't been screened that way.

If you're working with a physiotherapist, occupational therapist, or your wider Parkinson's team, it's worth telling them you're doing this. Many will know about motor imagery and can help you pair it with the exercises and strategies they've already given you.
