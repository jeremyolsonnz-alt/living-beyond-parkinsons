Steady Ground

A gentle mental rehearsal of standing easy and adjusting without alarm — the small, unhurried shifts of weight that steadiness is made of — practised safely in imagination, as a quiet companion to physiotherapy and a falls assessment.

Frequency  ·  Daily is a reasonable start Evidence  ·  Emerging — balance is a secondary finding only
Please read this first

The safety note that comes before everything else

Balance deserves more caution than any other practice on this site, so its safety note comes first rather than last. Postural instability is the Parkinson's symptom most closely tied to falls, and falls carry real consequences.

Do this practice seated or lying down, always. Never stand, shift your weight, or test your balance while doing it. This is imagination only. Picturing an easy weight-shift must never become an invitation to try one unsupported, and a sense of calm steadiness during the practice must never translate into attempting something you'd normally approach with care or with your usual support. If anything, treat this practice as a reason to be more deliberate about your real-world balance, not less.

Balance work belongs with a professional. The people who can actually help with postural instability are a physiotherapist and, where appropriate, a falls-assessment service — through prescribed balance training, home-hazard review, and the right walking aids. If falls or unsteadiness are part of your life, please ask your GP or neurologist for those referrals. This imagery practice is a quiet adjunct to that work, never a replacement for it, and never a substitute for a walking aid.

What this practice helps with

Postural instability

Postural instability — balance that feels less trustworthy, more effortful to hold — is one of the Parkinson's symptoms most closely tied to falls, and it's the practice on this site that carries the most caution. It also carries the thinnest evidence base of the five: there is no imagery study whose main purpose was to improve postural instability, and every balance signal here comes bundled inside research built for something else.

This practice rehearses the feeling of quiet, unhurried adjusting — not gripping or bracing against the world, but a relaxed, continuous settling, the small sway-and-correct that steadiness is actually made of. It's offered only ever seated or lying down, as a mental companion to the real work of physiotherapy and a falls assessment.

Shape of the session

The four parts of this practice

For why this method works and how the four parts fit together, see The Four Parts on the Guided Imagery page.

Part I · Breath and Light
What it's for

Anxiety about falling can itself make balance feel less trustworthy, so settling first is worth doing deliberately here — a calm body is closer to steady than a braced one.

How it works

A few unhurried minutes of settled breathing and a soft warmth low in the body — no balance imagery yet, just arriving, fully supported by the chair or bed.

Part II · The Ideal Being
What it's for

The figure isn't a memory of how you used to stand — there's no version of you to get back to. It's simply an embodiment of relaxed steadiness: adjusting without alarm, never a sudden save or catching a fall.

How it works

A figure is watched standing comfortably, with a small, constant sway and settle — unhurried, untroubled. Then the figure steps into you, so that ease of adjusting becomes yours from the inside, entirely in imagination.

Part III · The Balancing Body
What it's for

Understanding balance as a constant conversation, rather than a held position, makes the rehearsal make sense — and makes room for compassion when that conversation gets harder.

How it works

A plain-language look at how the feet, inner ear, and eyes constantly report to the brain, and how Parkinson's can slow that automatic answering — the real reason physiotherapy and a falls assessment matter so much.

A simplified schematic, not an anatomical illustration. Each line stands for many pathways running on both sides of the body, and balance draws on far more than the three sources shown. The real steadying happens on your feet, with your physiotherapist and your supports; this drawing only shows the conversation whose calm the practice rehearses.
Part IV · Closing
How it works

A slow return to the room, still fully supported by the chair or bed. Nothing to prove and no test passed or failed — and when you do rise, exactly as carefully as you always would, with whatever support you use.

The Script

Read this to yourself slowly — allow about 30–45 seconds per paragraph — or use it as the text for the audio narration above.

Read the Script

Part I — Breath and Light

And you're here now, held safely by the chair or the bed — nothing to hold up, nothing to steady. The surface beneath you is doing all the holding, completely, so that you can let go of doing it yourself.

Notice that. The way you are already, right now, entirely supported. There's a kind of steadiness in that — a steadiness you don't have to work for at all.

And the breath, arriving and leaving in its own quiet rhythm — itself a kind of gentle swaying, a small tide moving in and out, needing no correction, no effort. Just an easy, continuous motion that keeps itself.

A soft warmth somewhere easy — low in the body, perhaps, around the base of you, where we carry our sense of being grounded. Let it settle there, unhurried.

Part II — The Ideal Being

A little way off, there's a figure — standing. You may see them clearly, or simply sense that someone is there, standing comfortably, the way someone stands when they're not thinking about it at all. Not stiff, not braced, not guarding against anything. Simply upright, and at ease, weight resting down through the feet into the floor.

And notice something quiet happening. The figure is never perfectly still — no one is. There's a small, constant sway, and with it a small, constant settling: a little shift this way, an easy adjustment back, weight moving gently from one foot toward the other and back again. Unhurried. Untroubled. The body correcting itself the way it always has, softly, without alarm.

Notice that none of it is a scramble. Nothing is caught at the last moment. It's all slow and rolling and calm — sway and settle, shift and ease, like a boat resting on gentle water. This is the quality to stay with: not rigid stillness, but relaxed, flowing adjustment. Steadiness that breathes.

And now the figure turns, and comes closer, and steps into you — gently — so that being near it becomes a kind of knowing from the inside.

Take in, in imagination only, that easy uprightness. Weight resting down. A small, calm sway, and an easy settling with it — nothing gripped, nothing braced. Just the quiet, continuous adjusting that steadiness is really made of, rehearsed in the safety of your mind while your body rests. However your balance is today, you've taken in the ease of it, and that's yours to keep.

Part III — The Balancing Body

Staying upright is a quiet conversation the body holds with itself, all the time. Sensors in the feet and joints report where your weight is; the inner ear senses the tilt of your head; the eyes take in the level of the world. All of it streams to the brain, which answers with small, constant corrections — a little more weight here, a small adjustment there — mostly beneath any awareness.

In Parkinson's, some of that automatic answering can slow, and the corrections can become less sure. This is real, and it's why the practical work of a physiotherapist matters so much. What imagery can gently offer is a rehearsal of the calm version of this conversation — unhurried, trusting, without the tension that fear of falling can add on top.

So bring that quiet conversation to mind once more, running smoothly — the sensing, the small answering adjustments, the easy settling. Not forcing it. Just holding a sense of steadiness as something calm and continuous, so the calm is a little more familiar. The real steadying happens on your feet, with your physiotherapist and your supports; this simply keeps the calm of it close.

Part IV — Closing

And when you're ready, in your own time, let the room return — the chair or the bed still holding you completely. There is no need to rise quickly, and when you do rise, do it exactly as carefully as you always would, with whatever support you use.

Carry a little of the ease with you, however much came — the sense of steadiness as something calm and unhurried, rather than something to grip for. It's a feeling you've rehearsed, and it will be here again.

Your body moves through natural rhythms all day. About every 90 minutes, your mind drifts — when you notice that drift, you can return for a breath or two to the feeling of that calm, unhurried steadiness. At night, as you drift toward sleep, the practice is especially potent. A few breaths of it there carries the rehearsal into your dreams. The same is true when you first wake.

There was nothing to get back to here, and nothing to prove. This practice sits quietly beside the real work of balance — your physiotherapy, your falls assessment, your supports. It's a rehearsal of calm, offered to your body as it is today. And whenever you'd like it, the easy, steady ground is here to bring to mind again.

When to Practise

Once a day is a reasonable, sustainable starting point — there's no strict dose established for this practice, so let it fit wherever it fits in your day. The formal session isn't the only practice time; three other windows each day are also available.

Throughout the Day
Every 90 minutes or so, attention naturally softens — you might notice it as a dip in focus or a pull to pause. When you notice that drift, spend two or three breaths returning to the easy sway and settle of standing. It takes less than a minute and it counts.
Before Sleep
The minutes before sleep are when the mind is most receptive. Bring the feeling of calm, unhurried adjusting to those minutes — whatever you're holding in mind can carry on quietly working while you rest.
On Waking
This is often the most vulnerable transition point in the morning — a gentle reason to take your time getting up, not a claim that imagery itself reduces fall risk. A few unhurried moments here, before you're up, can be a calm way to begin.

An honest note. Balance carries the most cautious evidence badge of the five practices, and honestly so — there is no imagery study whose main purpose was to improve postural instability, and every balance signal here comes bundled inside research built for something else. In the pooled analyses of imagery and observation, balance appears as a secondary outcome with low-certainty improvement, and the same reviews include a well-conducted trial that found no effect on balance at all. The imagery-plus-virtual-reality trials reported gains in balance and balance-confidence, but imagery's own contribution there is unclear, since it was combined with VR. Full references are listed on the Bibliography page.

The specific four-part structure used here — Part I (breath and light), Part II (the Ideal Being), Part III (the Balancing Body), and Part IV (closing) — is Jeremy's own creation. Nobody has tested this exact combination in a clinical trial. Each piece on its own is backed by research; putting them together like this is a reasoned choice, not a proven method.

This is not a treatment. It's a free practice, meant to sit alongside — never replace — your physiotherapy, falls assessment, medication, and the guidance of your Parkinson's team. It is never a substitute for a walking aid or the caution your own balance calls for.
