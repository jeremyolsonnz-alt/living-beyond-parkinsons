Steady Ground

A gentle mental rehearsal of standing easy and adjusting without alarm — the small, unhurried shifts of weight that steadiness is made of — practised safely in imagination, as a quiet companion to physiotherapy and a falls assessment.

Emerging evidence · balance as a secondary finding only
Please read this first

The safety note that comes before everything else

Balance deserves more caution than any other practice on this site, so its safety note comes first rather than last. Postural instability is the Parkinson's symptom most closely tied to falls, and falls carry real consequences.

Do this practice seated or lying down, always. Never stand, shift your weight, or test your balance while doing it. This is imagination only. Picturing an easy weight-shift must never become an invitation to try one unsupported, and a sense of calm steadiness during the practice must never translate into attempting something you'd normally approach with care or with your usual support. If anything, treat this practice as a reason to be more deliberate about your real-world balance, not less.

Balance work belongs with a professional. The people who can actually help with postural instability are a physiotherapist and, where appropriate, a falls-assessment service — through prescribed balance training, home-hazard review, and the right walking aids. If falls or unsteadiness are part of your life, please ask your GP or neurologist for those referrals. This imagery practice is a quiet adjunct to that work, never a replacement for it, and never a substitute for a walking aid.

What this practice is for

Steadiness is made of small adjustments

We tend to think of balance as holding still. It's really the opposite: staying upright is a constant, quiet stream of tiny adjustments — the body swaying a little and correcting, shifting weight and settling, moment by moment, mostly below awareness. In Parkinson's, some of that automatic correcting can become slower or less sure, and standing can start to feel less trustworthy.

This practice rehearses the feeling of that quiet, unhurried adjusting — not gripping or bracing against the world, but a relaxed, continuous settling. It's a gentle mental picture of steadiness as something fluid and calm, held in imagination where it's completely safe to explore.

The practice

Around fifteen minutes, safely seated

Settle into a chair with a back, or lie down. You'll be picturing steadiness, not practising it on your feet — so you're completely secure throughout.

About the figure

Who the figure is — and isn't

In Phase I, a figure is seen standing comfortably and making small, easy adjustments of balance. As with every practice here, this figure is not you before Parkinson's, and not a steadier self to grieve or chase. There's no going back, and the practice never suggests it.

The figure is simply an embodiment of relaxed steadiness — the quality of standing without bracing, adjusting without alarm. When the figure steps into you, you borrow the feeling of that ease, and find it in your body as it is now. What you're rehearsing is calm, unhurried adjustment — never a sudden save, never catching a fall.

The full script

Read it, or read along

·Opening — Breath and Light▸

And you're here now, held safely by the chair or the bed — nothing to hold up, nothing to steady. The surface beneath you is doing all the holding, completely, so that you can let go of doing it yourself.

Notice that. The way you are already, right now, entirely supported. There's a kind of steadiness in that — a steadiness you don't have to work for at all.

And the breath, arriving and leaving in its own quiet rhythm — itself a kind of gentle swaying, a small tide moving in and out, needing no correction, no effort. Just an easy, continuous motion that keeps itself.

A soft warmth somewhere easy — low in the body, perhaps, around the base of you, where we carry our sense of being grounded. Let it settle there, unhurried.

IPhase I — The Ideal Being▸

A little way off, there's a figure — standing. You may see them clearly, or simply sense that someone is there, standing comfortably, the way someone stands when they're not thinking about it at all. Not stiff, not braced, not guarding against anything. Simply upright, and at ease, weight resting down through the feet into the floor.

And notice something quiet happening. The figure is never perfectly still — no one is. There's a small, constant sway, and with it a small, constant settling: a little shift this way, an easy adjustment back, weight moving gently from one foot toward the other and back again. Unhurried. Untroubled. The body correcting itself the way it always has, softly, without alarm.

Notice that none of it is a scramble. Nothing is caught at the last moment. It's all slow and rolling and calm — sway and settle, shift and ease, like a boat resting on gentle water. This is the quality to stay with: not rigid stillness, but relaxed, flowing adjustment. Steadiness that breathes.

And now the figure turns, and comes closer, and steps into you — gently — so that being near it becomes a kind of knowing from the inside.

Take in, in imagination only, that easy uprightness. Weight resting down. A small, calm sway, and an easy settling with it — nothing gripped, nothing braced. Just the quiet, continuous adjusting that steadiness is really made of, rehearsed in the safety of your mind while your body rests. However your balance is today, you've taken in the ease of it, and that's yours to keep.

IIPhase II — The Balancing Body▸

Staying upright is a quiet conversation the body holds with itself, all the time. Sensors in the feet and joints report where your weight is; the inner ear senses the tilt of your head; the eyes take in the level of the world. All of it streams to the brain, which answers with small, constant corrections — a little more weight here, a small adjustment there — mostly beneath any awareness.

In Parkinson's, some of that automatic answering can slow, and the corrections can become less sure. This is real, and it's why the practical work of a physiotherapist matters so much. What imagery can gently offer is a rehearsal of the calm version of this conversation — unhurried, trusting, without the tension that fear of falling can add on top.

So bring that quiet conversation to mind once more, running smoothly — the sensing, the small answering adjustments, the easy settling. Not forcing it. Just holding a sense of steadiness as something calm and continuous, so the calm is a little more familiar. The real steadying happens on your feet, with your physiotherapist and your supports; this simply keeps the calm of it close.

·Closing▸

And when you're ready, in your own time, let the room return — the chair or the bed still holding you completely. There is no need to rise quickly, and when you do rise, do it exactly as carefully as you always would, with whatever support you use.

Carry a little of the ease with you, however much came — the sense of steadiness as something calm and unhurried, rather than something to grip for. It's a feeling you've rehearsed, and it will be here again.

There was nothing to get back to here, and nothing to prove. This practice sits quietly beside the real work of balance — your physiotherapy, your falls assessment, your supports. It's a rehearsal of calm, offered to your body as it is today. And whenever you'd like it, the easy, steady ground is here to bring to mind again.

What the evidence says

Emerging — and the most cautious badge of the motor practices

Where the "Emerging" badge comes from

Balance carries the most cautious badge of the motor practices, and honestly so. There is no imagery study whose main purpose was to improve postural instability. Every balance signal comes bundled inside research built for something else.

In the pooled analyses of imagery and observation, balance appears as a secondary outcome with low-certainty improvement — and the same reviews include a well-conducted trial that found no effect on balance at all. The imagery-plus-virtual-reality trials reported gains in balance and balance-confidence, but those carry the same two problems noted throughout this site: imagery was combined with VR, so its own contribution is unclear, and balance was one outcome among several rather than the target.

So "Emerging" here means: a plausible, gentle adjunct with no direct evidence base of its own, resting on findings borrowed from other work. That is exactly why this page leans so hard on physiotherapy and a falls assessment as the real interventions, and offers imagery only as a quiet companion to them.

Full references — the pooled imagery/observation analyses, the null balance trial (Bezerra et al., 2022), and the imagery-plus-VR trials (Kashif et al., 2022) — are listed on the Bibliography page.

A note on this method

Jeremy's own synthesis

The four-part structure used across this programme — Opening, Phase I, Phase II, Closing, with the figure watched and then stepped into — is Jeremy's own synthesis, drawn from the Ericksonian and solution-oriented tradition he trained in. It has not been tested as a unified protocol in any clinical trial. The individual ingredients (motor imagery, action observation, breath work) have research behind them, as noted above; the particular way they're combined here is a considered personal design, offered in good faith, not a validated treatment.

Please treat this as a complement to your care, never a replacement for it. Guided imagery sits alongside physiotherapy, falls prevention, medication, and the guidance of your Parkinson's team — it does not stand in for any of them, and it is never a substitute for a walking aid or the caution your own balance calls for.
